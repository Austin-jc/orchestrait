00 · The trigger

A model of models, sold as one model

In June 2026, Sakana AI shipped Fugu: a multi-agent orchestration system delivered behind a single OpenAI-compatible endpoint. You send one request; internally a trained conductor decides whether to answer directly or assemble a team of specialist models — frontier APIs and open weights alike — and combine their work into one reply. Its top tier, Fugu Ultra, claims parity with Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos Preview while sidestepping export-control exposure, the pitch being AI sovereignty through swappable agents.

Two caveats frame everything below. The benchmarks are vendor-reported, and a "beats Opus" result from a system that calls Opus and synthesizes is a model-of-models win, not a single-model one. But the architecture is real, and grounded in two ICLR 2026 papers. This document is the reasoning we worked through to build our own — what the research says, which architectures exist, and what's worth copying.

Naming. Fugu's engine descends from two methods: TRINITY, a sub-20,000-parameter coordinator optimized by evolution, and the Conductor, a 7B model trained with reinforcement learning. The Conductor is the one with released code, released weights, and the headline results — so it anchors most of what follows.

01 · The map

Three levels of orchestrator

"Orchestrator" spans three genuinely different things. Only the third is a research artifact; the first two capture most of the practical value and need no training.

  • Router

    Classify the request, send it to the best single model. Heuristics, an embedding classifier, or a cheap LLM call. A gateway like LiteLLM normalizes providers behind one endpoint.

    effort · a weekend

  • Workflow orchestrator

    A plan → delegate → verify → synthesize loop. A controller decomposes the task, dispatches subtasks to workers, checks results, stitches an answer. LangGraph, CrewAI, or a hand-rolled state machine.

    effort · a couple of weeks

  • Learned orchestrator

    Train a small model to emit the coordination strategy. This is what the Conductor does, and where the difficulty — and the novelty — lives.

    effort · a research project
Steer

Unless training a router model is the actual point, a gateway plus a solid verify/synthesize loop reaches roughly 80% of what an orchestration product sells — on your own keys, shippable in weeks.

02 · The Conductor, in depth

A plan written as three lists

The Conductor's whole trick is that the coordination strategy is its output, emitted in a single completion before anything runs. After a chain-of-thought, it produces three index-aligned Python lists: which workers to call, what to ask each, and which earlier results each worker may see.

# one completion → a complete, executable DAG
model_id   = [ 2, 0 ]                    # which worker per step
subtasks   = [ "Devise an efficient algorithm…",
               "Implement the algorithm in Python" ]
access_list = [ [], ["all"] ]            # step-1 sees nothing; step-2 sees all prior

The access_list is the edge set: "all" wires a step to every prior message, [] runs it blind, an index list wires specific upstream steps. So the "tree" is really a DAG, and execution is just a loop — build each context from its access list, call the worker, store the result, return the last. Workers are passed as ordinal IDs (Model 0, Model 1…) so the planner explores capability rather than leaning on brand priors.

How it's trained

Pure reinforcement learning, DeepSeek-R1 style. The reward is blunt: zero if the three lists can't be parsed, 1 if executing the workflow yields the correct answer, 0.5 if it's well-formed but wrong. GRPO does the rest — coordination strategy emerges from end-to-end reward, never hand-designed.

IngredientWhat the paper used
Base modelQwen2.5-7B (a 3B variant also works, slightly weaker)
AlgorithmGRPO · no KL penalty · no reference-model sync
Training data960 verifiable problems (MATH500, MMLU, RLPR, LiveCodeBench v1)
Schedule200 iterations · batch 256 (4 questions × 64 rollouts)
WorkersGemini 2.5 Pro, Claude Sonnet 4, GPT-5 + open: DeepSeek-R1-32B, Gemma3-27B, Qwen3-32B
Compute2 × NVIDIA H100 80GB

The shock is how cheap the training is — two GPUs, a thousand problems, hours of work — because the cost has moved elsewhere: into the worker-API bill during rollouts, the async workflow-execution harness wired into the RL loop, and the verifiers that grade each domain. Compute isn't the moat; the rollout plumbing and the graders are.

What emerged

A 7B model coordinating bigger ones beat every individual worker and posted state-of-the-art numbers on LiveCodeBench and GPQA-Diamond. Two emergent behaviors matter for design: it became difficulty-adaptive — one or two steps for easy MMLU items, three or four for hard code generation — and it stayed frugal, averaging about three steps against a five-step budget with nothing regularizing it there. Allowed to call itself recursively, it gained a new test-time-scaling axis: read the first attempt, decide whether to revise.

03 · Where the value actually is

The ablations tell on the marketing

Three controlled cuts in the paper quietly relocate the value away from where you'd expect it.

The cutWhat it isolatesResult
Fix all workers to GPT-5Value of decomposition + prompt-engineering, with routing removedStill beats raw GPT-5 (69.8 vs 68.6)
Remove subtask textValue of tailored instructions vs bare model-selectionDrops everywhere, hardest on code (64→59)
Frontier model as conductorValue of the trained weights vs the scaffold itselfUntrained Gemini-as-conductor ≈ 71.6 of the 75.6

Read together: routing is the least of the three things bundled inside the orchestrator. Decomposition and verification topology carry real weight on their own, and a frontier model dropped into a good scaffold captures most of the gain without any training at all.

What training uniquely buys

Calibration. The untrained conductors leaned on prior beliefs about who's good at what — and those priors were wrong (they didn't know Claude was weak at LiveCodeBench) with no feedback to fix it. RL replaces guessed competence with measured competence.

Which reframes the whole "build vs train" question. The durable asset is neither the trained orchestrator nor a static "best-model-per-task" table — both rot the moment a model updates. The asset is the measurement apparatus: a harness that scores per-model, per-subtask success on verifiable problems. That's renewable, and it's the thing worth building.

Two loops, kept separate. The runtime loop answers a request. The measurement loop runs offline, scoring who wins each task so the runtime's "assign to a fitting model" step is calibrated rather than guessed. Conflating them is the common mistake.

04 · The architecture families

When does the plan get made?

Every agent architecture is an answer to three questions: when does it plan (before, during, or never), how many model calls per task, and how it handles failure. The Conductor is a plan-up-front design; its main rival plans step-by-step. Here is the field.

PatternMechanismPlansStrengthWeakness
ReActThink → act → observe → repeat, one step at a timeDuringAdaptive; recovers from surprisesMyopic; cost grows; can loop or drift
Plan-and-ExecutePlan all steps up front, execute sequentially, replan on failureBeforeFewer calls; inspectable before runningRigid to unexpected intermediate results
ReWOOPlan once with placeholders, run tools, synthesize at the endBefore~2 calls total; ~5× token efficiencyBreaks on an unexpected tool return
LLMCompilerEmit a DAG of tasks, execute independent branches in parallelBeforeLatency + cost via parallelismUp-front DAG must be largely correct
ADaPTPlan up front, recursively decompose a step only when it failsHybridAdapts exactly where neededNeeds a reliable per-step failure signal

The Conductor sits between ReWOO and LLMCompiler — a one-shot DAG with controlled visibility — with recursion as a coarse, whole-plan replan. ADaPT is the principled version of that recursion: keep the up-front DAG, but let a failing step spawn its own sub-plan instead of re-running everything.

The consistent trade — not a winner

Up-front planning wins on cost, inspectability, and parallelism but is rigid. Step-by-step (ReAct) wins on robustness in unpredictable environments but is myopic and expensive. The evidence on raw success rate is domain-dependent — one mobility benchmark gave ReAct a higher pass rate at a clear efficiency cost — so resist any universal claim.

What the frontier deep-research systems landed on is telling: both — a high-level up-front plan with local adaptive loops. That hybrid is the design worth stealing.

05 · The synthesis

The three aren't modes. They're a dial.

Single-pass DAG, recursive replan, and ReAct aren't a menu to choose from — they're low, medium, and high settings on one axis: how much adaptivity per unit of work. Seeing them this way dissolves the "pick a mode" problem, because a plan's shape already encodes its setting.

◦ low

Single-pass DAG

A flat plan, executed once. Cheapest, most inspectable. Right for tasks whose structure is knowable in advance.

◑ medium

Replan on failure

A step that fails its verifier spawns a local sub-plan. Adaptivity exactly where the evidence says it's needed.

● high

Scoped self-loop

A step observes, then decides — ReAct, but confined to the one piece that needs it, not the whole task.

The Conductor already does an embryonic version of this — allocating more steps to harder problems entirely on its own. Difficulty-adaptive behavior is mode-selection emerging from reward, in miniature. The proposed system (Doc 02) just widens the output language so the same emergent selection can reach further up the dial — and adds the two things that keep a dial from running away: hard budgets and verifiers as the trigger.
